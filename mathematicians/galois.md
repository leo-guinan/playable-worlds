## Évariste Galois — "Galois and the Games That Cannot Be Won"

### The Life

Évariste Galois was born on October 25, 1811, in Bourg-la-Reine, a small town just south of Paris, into a family of liberal republicans during the Bourbon Restoration — a period when French politics was conducted at close to maximum temperature. His father, Nicolas-Gabriel Galois, was the mayor of the town and an amateur poet. His mother, Adélaïde-Marie Demante, educated Évariste at home until he was twelve. The education was classical, literary, infused with republican politics, and it made him someone who connected mathematics and ideology in ways that would get him killed.

He entered the Lycée Louis-le-Grand in 1823. He was not, by the conventional measure, a successful student — he found most of his coursework boring, discovered mathematics late and explosively at fifteen, and became quickly impatient with the pace of formal instruction. He read Legendre's *Elements of Geometry* the way other teenagers read adventure novels, finishing it in days. He moved on to Lagrange's original papers. He was, at sixteen, doing mathematics that most professional mathematicians couldn't follow.

He applied twice to the École Polytechnique — the crown jewel of French mathematical education — and failed both times. The second attempt, by legend, ended with Galois throwing a blackboard eraser at an examiner who failed to understand his explanation. This may be apocryphal but has the texture of truth. What is certain is that he ended up at the École Normale Supérieure, a less prestigious institution, and that this humiliation marked him.

The personal disasters accelerated. In July 1829, his father — hounded by the local priest who forged letters in his name to discredit him politically — committed suicide. Évariste was eighteen. He submitted his work on polynomial equations to the Paris Academy twice; Cauchy apparently lost the first submission; the second was passed to Fourier, who died before reading it. The third submission was returned as incomprehensible by Poisson.

He threw himself into republican politics during the Revolution of 1830 and the turbulent years that followed. He was arrested twice — once for making a thinly veiled toast that was interpreted as a death threat against Louis-Philippe, once for wearing the uniform of the disbanded republican National Guard. He served time in prison, where he continued to develop his mathematics. His fellow prisoners, political idealists mostly, found him difficult and brilliant.

He was released in April 1832. On May 29, 1832, he wrote through the night — the famous letter to his friend Auguste Chevalier — laying out the complete structure of what is now called Galois theory, with margin notes that included the desperate aside: "I don't have time." The following morning, he was shot in the abdomen in a duel. He died the next day, May 31, 1832. He was twenty years old.

The cause of the duel is not entirely clear. The historical record includes a woman named Stéphanie, letters that Galois destroyed or she destroyed, and accounts suggesting it was arranged by political enemies or by a rival. What is clear is that Galois knew the outcome in advance — his letter has the quality of a man dictating from the edge of an abyss. He addressed mathematics at the point of death the way another man might address God.

His work was published fourteen years later, in 1846, by Liouville, who spent months convincing himself it was actually correct. It was. It was more than correct. It was foundational.

### The Mathematics (in human terms)

For two thousand years, mathematicians had been solving polynomial equations. You can solve quadratic equations (degree 2) with the quadratic formula. Cubic equations (degree 3) were cracked in the sixteenth century. Quartic equations (degree 4) fell shortly after. The natural question: is there a formula for degree 5 — the quintic?

Niels Henrik Abel had proved, just a few years before Galois, that there is no general formula using radicals (square roots, cube roots, etc.) for degree 5 or higher. But Abel's proof said: these games cannot be won. Galois asked: *why* not? And his answer transformed mathematics.

His insight was to look not at the equation itself but at the *symmetries* of its solutions — the ways you can rearrange the solutions without changing their relationships to each other. He showed that these symmetries form a structure (now called the Galois group of the equation), and that whether the equation is solvable by radicals depends on whether this group has a certain property (being "solvable," in the technical sense).

This was the invention of group theory. Galois took the question "can this game be won?" and replaced it with "what is the symmetry structure of this game?" — a move that generalized immediately to all of mathematics. Groups became the fundamental language for describing symmetry in physics, chemistry, and mathematics. Emmy Noether (Chapter 8) built on this. The Standard Model of particle physics is written in the language Galois invented.

The games that cannot be won are not failures. They are the games that reveal what's actually at stake. Galois showed that impossibility has structure, and structure is knowledge.

### Why This Chapter

Galois belongs here because he discovered that losing a game can be more informative than winning it — that the structure of impossibility is itself a mathematical object, one of the deepest. He is the founding figure of the idea that limitations have geometry.

### Reading List

1. **Mario Livio, *The Equation That Couldn't Be Solved* (2005)** — Best popular account of Galois and group theory; reads like a detective story; excellent on the mathematics and the life.

2. **Ian Stewart, *Galois Theory* (4th ed., 2015)** — The standard undergraduate textbook; more technical but written with unusual clarity and biographical sensitivity.

3. **Amir Alexander, *Duel at Dawn: Heroes, Martyrs, and the Rise of Modern Mathematics* (2010)** — Galois as a figure in the Romantic imagination of mathematics; excellent cultural context.

4. **Tony Rothman, "Genius and Biographers: The Fictionalization of Évariste Galois" (American Mathematical Monthly, 1982)** — The best historical debunking of the myths; essential for separating Galois from his legend.

5. **Joseph Rotman, *Galois Theory* (2nd ed., 1998)** — More accessible than Stewart at the introductory level; good for a reader who wants to understand the actual mathematics.

6. **Laura Toti Rigatelli, *Évariste Galois: 1811-1832* (1996)** — The most rigorous biography; based on archival research; essential for the duel and political context.

### Watch List

1. **"The Story of Évariste Galois" (Numberphile, YouTube, 2012)** — Peter Neumann (who edited the standard edition of Galois's manuscripts) talking about the man and the mathematics; authoritative and human.

2. **"Group Theory" (3Blue1Brown, YouTube, 2016–present)** — Grant Sanderson's series on group theory is the best visual introduction to what Galois invented; multiple videos.

3. **"Solving the Unsolvable: Galois Theory" (Royal Institution, YouTube)** — Accessible lecture on why the quintic can't be solved; builds from scratch.

4. **"Galois: The Passionate Mathematician" (ARTE documentary)** — French television documentary; available with subtitles; strong on the revolutionary political context.

### One Quote

*"I have done several new things in analysis. Some concern the theory of equations, others concern integral functions... In my life, I have often dared to advance propositions of which I was not sure. But all I have written here has been in my head for over a year, and it is too much in my interest not to deceive myself to be suspected of having announced theorems of which I did not have complete proofs."*

— Évariste Galois, letter to Auguste Chevalier, the night before the duel, May 29, 1832
