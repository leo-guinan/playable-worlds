## Thomas Schelling — "Schelling and the Discovery of Coordination"

### The Life

Thomas Crombie Schelling was born on April 14, 1921, in Oakland, California, the son of a naval officer. He grew up in ordinary American middle-class circumstances, moved frequently as military families do, and developed the observational disposition that would serve him so well later — he was a student of human behavior long before he formalized it.

He earned his bachelor's degree in economics from UC Berkeley in 1944, then spent time in postwar Europe working for the Marshall Plan, which gave him something most economists never get: direct observation of a continent trying to rebuild itself through a combination of rational incentives and irrational politics. He returned to get his PhD from Harvard in 1951. He spent most of his academic career at Harvard's economics department and Kennedy School, with an interlude at the RAND Corporation — the Cold War think tank where game theory was the coin of the realm — before finishing his career at the University of Maryland.

He was, by temperament and method, the opposite of the theoretical economist. He did not trust models that weren't grounded in observation. He was interested in what people actually did, which was frequently not what game theory predicted they would do. His great contribution was to figure out why the models were wrong — and to fix them.

The specific problem he worked on with the greatest urgency was nuclear strategy. He arrived at RAND and at Harvard's arms control seminars in the 1950s just as the hydrogen bomb had made the Cold War genuinely apocalyptic. The question that the strategists and economists and mathematicians were trying to answer was this: how do you have a nuclear standoff without nuclear war? How do you communicate resolve without triggering preemption? How do you make threats credible?

He answered these questions with ideas that were simultaneously rigorous and deeply human: people coordinate on focal points, on shared expectations, on visible limits that have no mathematical content but immense psychological reality. The thirty-eighth parallel was not the natural border of Korea; it was a focal point, a number round enough to be understood as a stopping place by both sides without communication. The nuclear taboo was not logically required; it was a bright line, a category distinction that served as a coordination device.

*The Strategy of Conflict* (1960) made him famous in a small world of people who thought about such things. *Micromotives and Macrobehavior* (1978) made him famous more broadly. The latter book contained his celebrated segregation model: show a population that prefers to have at least some neighbors of their own group — even a weak preference, well short of bigotry — and watch the model produce complete racial segregation. The micro-level preferences were mild; the macro-level outcome was total segregation. This result was disturbing and important and continues to be reproduced and debated.

He also worked on addiction, climate change, and the social determinants of health — always with the same method: start with what people actually do, find the coordination problem hiding underneath, model it carefully, and resist the temptation to assume rationality where something more complicated was operating.

He shared the Nobel Prize in Economics with Robert Aumann in 2005 "for having enhanced our understanding of conflict and cooperation through game-theory analysis." He was eighty-four.

He was known, throughout his career, as a brilliant lecturer who loved thought experiments, who could hold a seminar room in thrall with a scenario about hostages or nuclear arsenals or segregated housing, who had the rare gift of making social science feel urgent and alive. Former students recalled his courses as life-changing.

He died on December 13, 2016, at ninety-five, in Bethesda, Maryland. He had outlived most of the Cold War apparatus he had spent his career analyzing, and had the somewhat melancholy distinction of watching many of his ideas about nuclear stability be forgotten by a new generation of strategists.

### The Mathematics (in human terms)

Schelling's central insight is deceptively simple: in a world of multiple players with conflicting interests, the most important problem is often not optimization but coordination. Before you can ask what's best, you have to ask: how do we even get to the same page?

Consider the simplest version of his focal point idea. You and a stranger are asked to meet somewhere in New York City tomorrow at noon, but you have no way to communicate in advance. Where do you go? Game theory, in its standard form, says: any location is as good as any other, because without communication there's no way to solve the problem. People, in practice, go to Grand Central Terminal. Or the Empire State Building. Or Times Square. They find the focal point — the place that "suggests itself" by salience, by cultural convention, by being the kind of place people meet.

This is not irrational. It's a different kind of rationality — one that takes into account that reasoning happens inside a shared cultural context, that some points in the game space are more visible than others, that coordination requires not just individual optimization but shared expectations.

For nuclear strategy, this became the most important idea in arms control. The nuclear taboo — the norm against any nuclear use — has no formal logical basis. But it serves as a focal point: once any nuclear weapon is used, the coordination around "no nuclear weapons" is destroyed, and the next stopping point is unclear. The bright line has to be absolute to function as a coordination device. You can't have a policy of "just a small nuclear weapon." The coordination collapses.

The segregation model goes the other direction: it shows how coordination on a bad equilibrium — everyone sorting to their own group — can emerge from weak individual preferences, without any central direction, through local interactions. It's Nash equilibrium without any player trying to produce the macro-level outcome.

### Why This Chapter

Schelling belongs here because he discovered that the hardest game is not adversarial but coordinative — that the deepest problem in social life is often not winning against an opponent but finding the shared understanding that makes any game possible in the first place. He's the figure who shows that rules require culture, not just mathematics.

### Reading List

1. **Thomas Schelling, *The Strategy of Conflict* (1960)** — The founding text; still readable; the chapters on focal points and commitment are essential and accessible without technical background.

2. **Thomas Schelling, *Micromotives and Macrobehavior* (1978)** — Contains the segregation model and much else; Schelling at his most accessible; highly recommended.

3. **Richard Zeckhauser, ed., *Strategy and Conflict: An Introductory Sketch of Game Theory* (various)** — Schelling's influence on his students; good surrounding context.

4. **Avinash Dixit & Barry Nalebuff, *The Art of Strategy* (2008)** — Game theory for general readers; Schelling's ideas on commitment and coordination are central; well-written and practical.

5. **Robert Axelrod, *The Evolution of Cooperation* (1984)** — Builds directly on the coordination problem Schelling identified; shows how cooperation evolves in iterated prisoner's dilemmas.

6. **Poundstone, *Prisoner's Dilemma* (1992)** — The Cold War game theory context that makes Schelling's strategic insights legible; recommended alongside the Schelling primary texts.

### Watch List

1. **Thomas Schelling, Nobel Prize lecture (2005, YouTube)** — Schelling at eighty-four, still sharp; excellent on how coordination games shape the world.

2. **"Segregation Simulation" (Nicky Case, ncase.me/polygons)** — Interactive simulation of the Schelling segregation model; not video but essential; watching the model run is more illuminating than any lecture.

3. **"Dr. Strangelove" (Stanley Kubrick, film, 1964)** — Not about Schelling directly, but the RAND game-theory culture that Schelling inhabited is the film's target; watch it alongside *The Strategy of Conflict*.

4. **"Game Theory in the Real World" (RSA, YouTube, various)** — Several RSA lectures address Schelling's focal points and coordination games; David Halpern's lecture is particularly good.

5. **"The Fog of War" (Errol Morris, documentary, 2003)** — Robert McNamara on the Cuban Missile Crisis and Vietnam; the problems Schelling was trying to solve are vivid and human here.

### One Quote

*"People can often concert their intentions or expectations with others if each knows that the other is trying to do the same. Most situations — perhaps every situation — provide some clue for coordinating behavior, some focal point for each person's expectation of what the other expects him to expect to be expected to do."*

— Thomas Schelling, *The Strategy of Conflict*, 1960
