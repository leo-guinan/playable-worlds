## Bernhard Riemann — "Riemann and the Shape of Entire Worlds"

### The Life

Georg Friedrich Bernhard Riemann was born on September 17, 1826, in Breselenz, a tiny village in the Kingdom of Hanover, the second of six children of a Lutheran pastor. Friedrich Bernhard Riemann was poor, pious, and frail — the frailty ran through all the children, several of whom died young. Bernhard himself was shy, deeply religious, and prone to anxiety so severe that in adulthood he suffered apparent nervous breakdowns. He was also, in the quiet and unostentatious way of genuinely extraordinary people, almost certainly the most creative geometer in human history.

His father, recognizing his son's gifts early, initially taught him himself and then scraped together money for schooling. At Gymnasium in Lüneburg, Riemann read Legendre's number theory — borrowed from the director — in six days and returned it, saying he had mastered it. The director tested him. He had.

He entered the University of Göttingen in 1846 intending to study theology — a continuation of the family vocation — but switched to mathematics with his father's blessing after a single semester. He studied under Gauss and under the physicist Wilhelm Weber. He then spent time at Berlin, where he encountered Dirichlet and Jacobi. The German mathematical tradition that shaped him was rigorous, philosophical, and intensely interested in the relationship between geometry and physics — a combination that proved decisive.

His career was a slow accumulation of masterworks produced under constant financial anxiety and deteriorating health. He submitted his doctoral dissertation in 1851, which introduced what are now called Riemann surfaces. His Habilitation lecture in 1854 — the lecture a young German academic gave to demonstrate readiness for a professorship — was "On the Hypotheses That Lie at the Foundations of Geometry," a thirty-page paper that effectively invented differential geometry and, incidentally, provided the mathematical framework that Einstein would use fifty years later for general relativity. Gauss, who was in the audience at 77 and rarely praised anyone, was visibly moved.

He published the 1859 paper "On the Number of Primes Less Than a Given Magnitude" — eight pages, the most important paper in number theory — in which he introduced the Riemann zeta function and stated, almost as an aside, the conjecture that all non-trivial zeros of the function lie on the critical line with real part 1/2. This is the Riemann Hypothesis. It is still unproven. The Clay Mathematics Institute offers a million-dollar prize for its resolution. One hundred sixty-five years on, no one has won it.

His personal life was one of intermittent happiness surrounded by loss. He cared for his sisters, helped support his brother, lived cheaply in Göttingen. He married Elise Koch in 1862; their daughter Ida was born in 1863. The marriage was apparently genuinely loving, which makes what followed worse. His health, never strong, collapsed after his first trip to Italy in 1862 — he contracted pleurisy, which developed into tuberculosis. He spent his last years making trips to Italy hoping the climate would help, growing weaker. He died on July 20, 1866, in Selasca on Lake Maggiore, in the middle of working on a paper. He was thirty-nine.

His housekeeper, following German custom, burned many of his papers after his death. What survived is enough to have reshaped physics and mathematics. What was lost — we don't know. That's its own kind of horror.

### The Mathematics (in human terms)

Before Riemann, geometry meant Euclid: flat planes, parallel lines that never meet, the angles of a triangle sum to 180 degrees. This is the geometry of billiard tables and graph paper.

Riemann's 1854 lecture asked a different question: what if space itself has a shape? What if the geometry of the world is not flat but curved — and what if different regions of space can have different curvatures?

He invented the mathematical language to answer this question. A Riemannian manifold is a space where, at every point, you have a local notion of distance that can vary from place to place. On the surface of a sphere, the shortest path between two points is a great circle, not a straight line. The geometry is intrinsically curved — you can detect the curvature without leaving the surface, just by measuring angles and distances.

This is "the shape of entire worlds" — not just metaphorically. General relativity, Einstein's theory of gravity, is essentially the statement that spacetime is a Riemannian manifold whose curvature is determined by the distribution of mass and energy. The shape of space is not a backdrop to physics; it *is* physics. Riemann gave us the mathematical vocabulary to say this fifty years before Einstein needed it.

The Riemann Hypothesis lives in this same spirit: it says that the distribution of prime numbers — which seems chaotic and random — is actually controlled by an elegant structure in the complex plane. The zeros of the zeta function encode information about where the primes are. The primes, like space, have a shape. Finding it is the unsolved game at the heart of mathematics.

### Why This Chapter

Riemann belongs here because he made the shape of space into a mathematical problem — he discovered that the world itself is a game board with geometry, and that understanding the board is prior to playing on it. He also gave us the most famous unsolved problem in mathematics, the ultimate hidden game.

### Reading List

1. **John Derbyshire, *Prime Obsession: Bernhard Riemann and the Greatest Unsolved Problem in Mathematics* (2003)** — The best popular account of the Riemann Hypothesis; alternates chapters of biography with chapters of mathematics; genuinely illuminating.

2. **Marcus du Sautoy, *The Music of the Primes* (2003)** — More narrative than Derbyshire; du Sautoy is a beautiful writer and the book has a strong story arc.

3. **Detlef Laugwitz, *Bernhard Riemann 1826–1866: Turning Points in the Conception of Mathematics* (1999)** — The serious biography; essential for the mathematical content.

4. **Bernhard Riemann, "On the Hypotheses Which Lie at the Bases of Geometry" (1854, translated in various collections)** — The lecture itself; short and readable even if the mathematics is dense; the prose is remarkable.

5. **Brian Greene, *The Elegant Universe* (1999)** — Not about Riemann directly, but the best popular account of Riemannian geometry's role in physics; makes the stakes clear.

6. **Barry Mazur & William Stein, *Prime Numbers and the Riemann Hypothesis* (2016)** — Aimed at general readers; uses visual representations to make the hypothesis accessible without requiring advanced mathematics.

### Watch List

1. **"The Riemann Hypothesis" (Numberphile, YouTube, multiple videos)** — The series of videos with various mathematicians explaining the hypothesis and why it matters; start with the 2016 video with Edward Frenkel.

2. **"Visualizing the Riemann Zeta Function" (3Blue1Brown, YouTube, 2016)** — Grant Sanderson's visual walkthrough of complex analysis; the best way to see what the zeta function actually does.

3. **"What is the Riemann Hypothesis?" (Quanta Magazine video, YouTube)** — Short, clear explainer; good starting point before the deeper dives.

4. **"Einstein's Field Equations" (PBS Space Time, YouTube)** — Makes the connection between Riemannian geometry and general relativity concrete; essential for understanding Riemann's physical legacy.

### One Quote

*"It is well known that geometry presupposes not only the concept of space but also the first fundamental notions for constructions in space as given in advance. It gives only nominal definitions for them, while the essential means of determining them appear in the form of axioms. The relationship of these presuppositions is left in the dark; one sees neither whether and how far their connection is necessary, nor a priori whether it is possible."*

— Bernhard Riemann, opening of "On the Hypotheses Which Lie at the Foundations of Geometry," 1854
