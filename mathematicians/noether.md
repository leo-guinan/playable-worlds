## Emmy Noether — "Noether and the Laws That Cannot Be Broken"

### The Life

Amalie Emmy Noether was born on March 23, 1882, in Erlangen, Bavaria, into an unusual family. Her father Max Noether was a professor of mathematics at the University of Erlangen — already unusual in nineteenth-century Germany, where university positions were hard-won affairs — and a practicing mathematician of real distinction. Emmy grew up in a household where mathematics was the ambient medium. She and her brother Fritz both became mathematicians.

She did not, initially, seem destined for anything out of the ordinary. By her own account, she was a competent but unremarkable student, more interested in languages and dancing than mathematics until well into her teens. She passed her teaching exam in French and English, which was the conventional path for educated German women who wanted something like a professional life. Then something shifted — precisely what is unclear — and she turned to mathematics.

She was not allowed to formally enroll at the University of Erlangen. Women could audit lectures with the professor's permission but could not matriculate. She audited for two years, then passed the matriculation exam at the University of Göttingen as an external student, then returned to Erlangen, which had relaxed its rules by 1904. She received her doctorate in 1907 under Paul Gordan, on a highly technical problem in invariant theory. She later called it "crap" — she had mastered the computational methods of the old school and then, in her own maturation, moved past them.

For seven years after her doctorate, she worked at the Mathematical Institute in Erlangen without pay, without position, without title. She taught her father's courses when he was ill. She published. She developed. The exclusion was complete and apparently accepted — not contentedly, but as the structure of the world. In 1915, David Hilbert and Felix Klein invited her to Göttingen, which was the center of world mathematics. Even they could not get her a formal position. When Hilbert tried to have her admitted to the faculty, the historian colleagues objected. His response is remembered: "I do not see that the sex of the candidate is an argument against her admission as a *Privatdozent*. After all, we are a university, not a bathing establishment."

She was admitted — as his assistant, unpaid, lecturing under his name. She was allowed to collect fees from students who attended her lectures. From 1915 to 1933, she worked in this essentially extralegislative capacity, without formal salary or title, at the institution that produced more major mathematicians than anywhere else in the world. She was acknowledged, respected, even celebrated — Felix Klein called her work indispensable — but the formal structures refused to accommodate her.

The students she gathered around her — the "Noether boys," as they were called, with no irony intended — idolized her. She lectured by walking around the room thinking out loud, working through problems in real time, expecting her students to keep up. She was generous with ideas, notoriously careless about personal possessions, politically progressive, Jewish, and utterly indifferent to the trappings of academic rank that she had been denied anyway.

In 1933, the Nazis came to power. Noether, Jewish and associated with the political left, was among the first academics dismissed under the Law for the Restoration of the Professional Civil Service. She was fifty-one. She went to Bryn Mawr College in Pennsylvania — one of the women's colleges that opened its doors to Jewish refugee academics — and to the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton. She was finally, improbably, in institutions that valued her and paid her.

She died on April 14, 1935, of complications following surgery for an ovarian cyst. She was fifty-two. The eulogies that followed — from Einstein, from Hermann Weyl, from Albert Einstein — were extraordinary in their recognition. Einstein wrote in the New York Times that she was "the most significant creative mathematical genius thus far produced, so far as women are concerned" — a sentence that even in praise insisted on the qualifier. Weyl, more honest, said simply that she was "a great mathematician."

The navigation of exclusion she had done for her entire career was not rage and it was not submission — it was something harder: pure focus. She treated the absence of institutional validation as irrelevant to the work, which was the only thing that mattered anyway. Whether this was wisdom or trauma or both is impossible to say from the outside.

### The Mathematics (in human terms)

In 1915, when Noether arrived in Göttingen, Hilbert and Einstein were trying to understand general relativity. Specifically, they were troubled by a conservation question: in Newtonian physics, energy is always conserved. But in general relativity, the very notion of energy seemed unstable — in curved spacetime, was energy still conserved or not?

Noether resolved this in a single theorem, published in 1918, that is now recognized as one of the deepest results in all of physics. Noether's theorem states: *every continuous symmetry of a physical system corresponds to a conserved quantity.*

If a system's laws are the same today as they were yesterday — if the laws are symmetric in time — then energy is conserved. If the laws are the same here as they are over there — spatial symmetry — then momentum is conserved. If the laws are symmetric under rotation — the same in every direction — then angular momentum is conserved.

The symmetry comes first. The conservation law follows from it, necessarily. The laws that cannot be broken are not arbitrary — they are the fingerprints of the symmetries of the universe.

This is staggering. It means that conservation laws — some of the most empirically robust facts we know about physics — are not additional assumptions about reality. They are consequences of symmetry. They emerge from the shape of the game.

Her work in abstract algebra — the structure theory of rings, ideals, and modules — was equally foundational and is harder to explain briefly. She reorganized algebra around structures rather than computations, which is the move that made modern mathematics possible. Galois had given us groups; Noether showed how to think algebraically about everything.

### Why This Chapter

Noether belongs here because she discovered that the deepest laws of physics are not imposed on the universe from outside — they emerge from symmetry, from the structure of the game itself. She is also the figure on this list who most clearly demonstrates that exclusion from the arena does not prevent the work; it just makes the achievement more expensive.

### Reading List

1. **Ransom Stephens, *The God Proof* (2010)** — Novel; not biography, but excellent on what it felt like to be in Noether's circle. A more unusual recommendation that works surprisingly well.

2. **Emmy Noether, *Noether: Gesammelte Abhandlungen / Collected Papers* (Springer, 1983)** — Her collected works; the introduction by Nathan Jacobson is the best short account of her mathematical contributions.

3. **Dwight E. Neuenschwander, *Emmy Noether's Wonderful Theorem* (2011)** — The best book-length treatment of Noether's theorem accessible to non-physicists; requires calculus but is beautifully written.

4. **Hermann Weyl, "Emmy Noether" (eulogy, 1935, reprinted in various collections)** — Weyl's memorial address is one of the great short portraits of a mathematician by a peer; read it.

5. **Lee Phillips, "Emmy Noether's Theorem" (Ars Technica, 2016)** — Long-form article; excellent contemporary treatment; free online.

6. **Leon M. Lederman & Christopher T. Hill, *Symmetry and the Beautiful Universe* (2004)** — Accessible physics book that makes Noether's theorem central; good context for understanding the physical stakes.

### Watch List

1. **"Emmy Noether: The Most Important Mathematician You've Never Heard Of" (PBS Space Time, YouTube, 2018)** — Excellent overview of both the life and Noether's theorem; strong on the physics implications.

2. **"Noether's Theorem" (3Blue1Brown mentioned but Minutephysics, YouTube, 2013)** — Short and clear; starts with symmetry and derives conservation; remarkable how much it gets across in four minutes.

3. **"The Most Beautiful Idea in Physics" (Quanta Magazine, YouTube, 2021)** — Extended treatment of Noether's theorem with multiple physicists explaining why they consider it foundational.

4. **"Women in Mathematics" (IAS, Institute for Advanced Study, YouTube)** — Panel discussion including Noether's legacy; features contemporary mathematicians talking about what her navigation of exclusion meant.

### One Quote

*"My methods are really methods of working and thinking; this is why they have crept in everywhere anonymously."*

— Emmy Noether
